For Filipino students, 16 to 25 Street + online harassment

Safe on the street. Safe on the screen.

Catcalling, stalking and online harassment are everyday realities for many young Filipinos. They are also against the law. Learn to spot them, stop them and report them.

What counts as harassment

Gender-based sexual harassment is any unwanted remark, gesture or act aimed at someone because of their sex, gender, gender expression or sexual orientation. Philippine law recognizes that it happens on streets, in jeepneys and trains, at school, at work and online.13

It is not about how someone dresses or where they walk. It is about a person choosing to make someone else feel unsafe.

Forms it takes

  • Catcalling and wolf-whistling

    Whistles, "hi sexy," kissing sounds or comments shouted at someone passing by.

  • Stalking and following

    Following someone home, waiting outside their school, or repeatedly showing up where they are.

  • Unwanted comments

    Remarks about a person's body, sexual jokes, or relentless questions about their name, number or destination.

  • Online harassment

    Unwanted sexual messages, threats, sharing photos without consent, or spreading sexual rumors on social media.

The law protects you

Five Philippine laws work together against harassment and gender-based violence. Open each one for what it covers, what it costs offenders, and the full text.

  1. Covers: sexual harassment by someone with authority or influence over you at work, in school or in training, such as a boss, teacher or coach who demands sexual favors.

    Penalty: 1 to 6 months in jail, a fine of ₱10,000 to ₱20,000, or both.

    Read the full text on LawPhil [9]

  2. Covers: physical, sexual, psychological and economic abuse of women and their children by a husband, ex-partner, or someone they are dating or have dated, including stalking and harassment by that person.

    Penalty: jail terms that rise with the seriousness of the act, a fine of ₱100,000 to ₱300,000, and required counseling. Survivors can also get protection orders.

    Read the full text on LawPhil [10]

  3. Covers: the rights of women to equality and to protection from all forms of violence. It requires government offices to set up gender programs and violence-against-women desks.

    Penalty: it is a rights law rather than a criminal law. Officials and institutions who violate it face administrative sanctions and can be ordered to pay damages.

    Read the full text on LawPhil [11]

  4. Covers: crimes committed through computers and the internet, including cybersex, online libel and illegal access to someone's accounts.

    Penalty: crimes under the Revised Penal Code carry a penalty one degree higher when committed online, on top of the specific penalties the act sets for cybercrimes.

    Read the full text on LawPhil [12]

  5. Covers: gender-based sexual harassment in streets, public spaces, public transport, online, at work and in schools, including harassment between peers and by strangers. Homophobic and transphobic slurs are explicitly included.

    Penalty in public spaces (grows with each repeat offense):

    • Catcalling, wolf-whistling, slurs, persistent comments: first offense ₱1,000 plus 12 hours of community service and a gender sensitivity seminar; up to 30 days in jail and ₱10,000 for a third offense.
    • Offensive gestures and flashing: first offense ₱10,000 plus community service; jail time for repeat offenses.
    • Stalking, groping and unwanted touching: first offense 11 to 30 days in jail or ₱30,000; up to 6 months and higher fines for repeat offenses.

    Penalty online: prisión correccional (medium period) or a fine of ₱100,000 to ₱500,000.

    Read the full text on LawPhil [13] [14]

It affects all of us

Most reported harassment targets women and girls, but it does not stop there. Men are harassed too, and LGBTQIA+ people are often targeted precisely because of how they look, love or express themselves.6 The Safe Spaces Act protects every gender and names homophobic and transphobic slurs as harassment.13

Girls and young women who are LGBTIQ+, from an ethnic minority or living with a disability face even higher risk online.4 Empowerment means making every space safe for all of them.

If you see it: the 5 D's

Five ways a bystander can help. Use whichever feels safe for you in the moment.17

  • D

    Distract

    Interrupt without confronting. Ask the person for directions or the time, or pretend you know them.

  • D

    Delegate

    Get help from someone with authority: the jeepney driver, a guard, a teacher, or a barangay tanod.

  • D

    Document

    If someone is already helping and it's safe, record or take notes. Never post it without the person's consent.

  • D

    Delay

    Check in afterward. Ask "Are you okay?" and offer to walk with them or help them report.

  • D

    Direct

    Only if it's safe: say firmly "That's not okay, leave them alone," then turn your attention back to the person.
